[0:30 - 1:15] SLIDE 2: Problem & Motivation (5G RAN Slicing Dilemma)
"On Slide 2, we present the problem and motivation. In 5G networks, network slicing partitions physical cellular base stations so multiple mobile operators can run distinct services—such as video on YouTube, web browsing on Google, and social media on Facebook and Instagram. To guarantee quality of service without over-allocating costly radio spectrum, the network must forecast traffic demand minutes in advance. 

However, centralized machine learning requires pooling raw user traffic counters on a central server. This violates user privacy laws like GDPR and exposes commercial secrets to competitors. Conversely, training isolated local models at each base station—called DLSTM—fails completely because local towers lack sufficient historical data and overfit during unexpected traffic spikes. This creates the central dilemma: how can we achieve accurate multi-slice forecasts without exposing private traffic data?"

[1:15 - 1:55] SLIDE 3: System Model & Network Architecture
"On Slide 3, the authors propose a federated learning architecture. Physical base stations act as local clients, training on-device and transmitting only model parameters to an edge coordinator. Equation 1 formalizes this as an empirical risk minimization objective where local losses are weighted by local sample counts. Each station utilizes a Long Short-Term Memory network. We verified Equation 2: with 64 hidden units, 5 past observation steps, and 1 prediction step, each model contains exactly 17,729 parameters, requiring just 69 kilobytes per update."

[1:55 - 2:30] SLIDE 4: Methodology: Algorithms 1 to 4 Breakdown
"On Slide 4, we examine Algorithms 1 through 4. Algorithm 1 handles the federated communication loop. Algorithm 2 runs local FedProx training with a proximal regularization term to prevent local models from diverging under non-identical traffic distributions. Algorithm 3 introduces Intelligent Clustering using PCA and K-means, and Algorithm 4 provides a balanced Random Clustering baseline."

[2:30 - 3:00] SLIDE 5: Experimental Setup & Calibrated Dataset
"On Slide 5, we detail our experimental setup. Because the authors' French Orange dataset is proprietary, we built a cellular traffic generator faithfully calibrated to the paper's parameters: 57 base stations, 4 application slices, and 10 days of traffic at 10-minute resolution, split chronologically into 7 days training, 1 day validation, and 2 days testing across 3 random seeds. 

Now, Vihaan will present our reproduction results."

[3:00 - 3:45] SLIDE 6: Baseline Reproduction: Centralized vs. DLSTM
"Thank you, Ankush. Hello everyone, I am Vihaan Gupta, roll number CS24B1063, Member 2 and the Reproduction Lead.

Turning to Slide 6, we examine our baseline benchmarks across 3 random seeds. Centralized LSTM, where all cellular data is pooled centrally, achieves an average Test Root Mean Square Error of 0.0712, setting the empirical performance upper bound. In stark contrast, decentralized DLSTM—where 228 local models train in complete isolation—suffers an average error of 0.2039. That is an enormous 186% error penalty. Without collaborative learning, local cellular towers simply lack enough data samples to generalize across bursty traffic slices, proving that isolated on-device training is unacceptable."

[3:45 - 4:30] SLIDE 7: Federated Learning Reproduction: FedAvg vs FedProx
"On Slide 7, we evaluate the paper's collaborative approaches: FedAvg, called FALSTM, and FedProx, called FPLSTM. Both keep private traffic strictly on local base stations, training for 1 local epoch per round using Adam. FedAvg achieves an RMSE of 0.1748, slashing local DLSTM error by 14.3% through weight aggregation. True FedProx, configured with proximal hyperparameter mu equals 1.0, achieves an RMSE of 0.1757, delivering the lowest variance across seeds and stabilizing training under traffic heterogeneity. 

Crucially, we also implemented the paper's literal Algorithm 2 as written, where proximal regularization is applied post-hoc after local Adam optimization. The paper's literal Algorithm 2 produced an RMSE of 0.1850—a 5.3% performance penalty. This empirically confirms our audit finding: proximal regularization must be applied inside local gradient steps to prevent client drift."

[4:30 - 5:15] SLIDE 8: Hyperparameter Analysis: Proximal Regularization mu
"On Slide 8, we reproduce the paper's Figure 12, sweeping the proximal parameter mu from 0.001 to 1.0 under 50% client participation. As mu increases, the proximal pull toward the global model prevents local drift, monotonically reducing forecasting error from 0.1804 down to 0.1743. In highly diverse cellular environments, a strong proximal weight of 1.0 provides superior stability."

[5:15 - 6:00] SLIDE 9: Client Participation Omega & Communication Cost
"On Slide 9, we examine client participation Omega from 10% to 100%, alongside communication overhead using the paper's cost model: 2 times rounds, times stations, times participation, times model size. Increasing participation from 10% to 30% drops RMSE sharply from 0.2517 down to 0.1810. Above 50%, performance plateaus, but communication payload scales strictly linearly, from 18 up to 185 megabytes. Because model size is only 69 kilobytes, federated learning transmits 99% less data than centralizing raw cellular telemetry. This demonstrates that 50% participation is the optimal operational sweet spot, capturing 98% of peak accuracy while cutting wireless backhaul transmission by half.

Now, Piyush will discuss clustered federated learning and walk us through our critical audit."

[6:00 - 6:45] SLIDE 10: Clustered Federated Learning Performance
"Thank you, Vihaan. Hello everyone, I am Piyush Mishra, roll number CS24B1048, Member 3 and the Scalability Lead.

On Slide 10, we evaluate Clustered Federated Learning with 3 clusters. The authors reduce daily traffic curves to 2 principal components explaining 91% variance, claiming that grouping stations by traffic trend dramatically outperforms random clustering. However, our multi-seed reproduction reveals a different reality: trend-based intelligent clustering achieves an RMSE of 0.2149, while our fixed balanced random clustering achieves 0.2176. The actual performance gain of trend clustering over random clustering is merely 1.24%! Furthermore, clustering simply by latitude and longitude achieves 0.2116 without using any private traffic data."

[6:45 - 7:35] SLIDE 11: Critical Audit: Algorithmic & Mathematical Flaws
"On Slide 11, we present our audit of algorithmic and mathematical flaws. First, in Algorithm 2, Line 8 runs Adam for local epochs without a proximal term, and Line 9 performs an undefined post-hoc minimization. This contradicts true FedProx and degrades accuracy by 5.3%. Second, Equation 1 specifies a sample-weighted global loss, yet Algorithm 1 Line 7 computes an unweighted arithmetic mean, biasing convergence when base stations have different traffic volumes. Third, Algorithm 4 contains a fatal bug: it checks cluster size against set B_s divided by K while simultaneously removing elements from B_s inside the loop! This dynamically shrinks the threshold, prematurely terminating cluster 1 after just 12 stations instead of 19, and dumping 33% of base stations into a bloated surplus cluster. Our corrected implementation precomputes the fixed target size upfront."

[7:35 - 8:20] SLIDE 12: Critical Audit: Experimental Rigor & Fair Comparison
"On Slide 12, we audit experimental rigor. The paper has a severe notation swap in Section 6-E, reversing prediction window H and observation window W. Its evaluation is thin: only 1 city, 10 days of data, zero random seeds, no error bars, and hyperparameters tuned directly on test data without an explicit validation split. Additionally, its comparison with DeepCog is unfair, because DeepCog optimizes an asymmetric capacity loss, yet was evaluated only on symmetric RMSE. The paper's Table V confirms that clustering gains are only 0.4% to 1.5%."

[8:20 - 9:00] SLIDE 13: Critical Audit: Privacy Guarantees & Realism
"Finally, on Slide 13, we expose critical architectural flaws. Most importantly, the paper suffers a privacy paradox: to cluster by trend in Algorithm 3, base stations must upload 24-hour raw traffic vectors to the coordinator, completely violating the core premise of federated learning! Furthermore, forcing 4 heterogeneous application slices into one global model causes negative transfer between video and social media traffic, while runtime comparisons excluded clustering overhead.

Now, Narayana Murthy will present our four validated improvements."

[9:00 - 9:40] SLIDES 14 & 15: Improvements A & B (Weighted Aggregation & Private Clustering)
"Thank you, Piyush. Hello everyone, I am Narayana Murthy S, roll number CS24B1047, Member 4 and the Extension Lead.

To resolve the paper's limitations, our team designed, implemented, and verified four technical improvements across 3 random seeds.

On Slide 14, Improvement A fixes the mathematical aggregation mismatch. We implemented exact sample-weighted FedProx matching Equation 1. When evaluating base stations with variable data availability, sample-weighted aggregation reduced test RMSE from 0.2632 to 0.2434—an average 7.5% error reduction over 3 seeds, with gains up to 11.7% on individual seeds.

On Slide 15, Improvement B resolves the privacy violation in clustering. Instead of sharing raw traffic telemetry, base stations perform one local warmup round and share ephemeral model weight updates. The server clusters stations using cosine similarity over these weights. This achieves a competitive RMSE of 0.2777 while completely eliminating raw traffic leakage, preserving operational confidentiality and solving the privacy paradox."

[9:40 - 10:20] SLIDES 16 & 17: Improvements C & D (SLA Loss & Differential Privacy)
"On Slide 16, Improvement C addresses SLA violations in production slicing. Standard Mean Squared Error penalizes under-prediction and over-prediction equally. But in cellular networks, under-prediction causes dropped calls and SLA breaches. We introduced an asymmetric Pinball Loss with tau equals 0.8, penalizing under-prediction four times more heavily. Tested across margins gamma from 0 to 50 percent, pinball loss slashed SLA violations from 21.8% down to 15.0%—a dramatic 31% relative reduction in contract violations with zero penalty on overall RMSE.

On Slide 17, Improvement D establishes formal Differential Privacy using gradient clipping and Gaussian noise injection. Sweeping noise sigma from 0 to 0.1 proves that sigma equals 0.005 provides strong privacy protection against reconstruction attacks while maintaining high accuracy at 0.2086 RMSE."
